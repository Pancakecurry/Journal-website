# Campus Atelier Design Language

## Direction

Campus Atelier is a beautifully lived-in university desk turned into a visual world. It draws inspiration from Japanese planner restraint, Korean study-stationery warmth, contemporary campus culture, editorial design, and an exceptionally organized personal desk.

These are references for restraint, paper craft, flexible organization, color, and personalization—not permission to imitate cultural stereotypes, use fake Japanese/Korean writing, or produce a generic “Asian study aesthetic.”

The design is **quietly expressive**: calm, prepared, warm, optimistic, polished, human, personal without mess, collectible without gamification, and cute only in very small doses.

## Design intensity: Desk → Notebook → Paper

| Mode | Use | Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expressive / Desk | Today, My World, onboarding, monthly recaps, playful browsing | Environmental composition, layered materials, one or two meaningful objects |
| Organized / Notebook | Calendar, Journal library, trackers, search, categorized settings | Tabs, grids, rules, labels, and restrained structure |
| Focused / Paper | Journal writing, reflection, editing, focused reading | Decoration recedes; typography, content, and concentration lead |

The more concentration a task requires, the quieter the interface becomes. Modes should feel related, as though moving closer to the paper on the same desk.

## Material language

Use warm-white notebook paper, cool-white sheets, cream paper, ruled paper, dot and graph grids, translucent tabs, matte dividers, binder labels, highlighter ink, sticky notes, bookmarks, clips, separators, laminated covers, occasional textile-like texture, stamps, date markings, and small annotations.

The system should make content feel capable of being **placed, stacked, clipped, filed, turned, highlighted, tucked away, or pulled out again**. Material cues must clarify hierarchy and spatial behavior, not merely add beige texture.

## Color

The foundation is warm paper, soft neutral surfaces, and dark ink. Restrained stationery accents may include powder blue, sage, lavender, butter yellow, soft peach, coral, and mint. Several accents can coexist when coordinated.

Avoid pastel overload, bright app gradients, oversaturated technology-brand colors, low-contrast “aesthetic” text, and color as the only carrier of meaning.

## Typography

Combine:

- Friendly editorial headings
- Clean functional interface text
- Highly readable long-form typography
- Precise dates and numeric typography

Small handwriting-like accents may appear as rare personal flourishes. Never use handwriting for primary UI or long-form reading. The target is a well-designed notebook plus contemporary editorial typography—not handwritten diary software.

## Composition and decoration

Prefer one charming environmental detail over many stickers. A mug, pen, plant, cassette, Polaroid, paperclip, books, bookmark, pencil case, or desk clock may provide character when it has a compositional or personal role.

Layering should retain clear focus order, usable hit targets, readable text, and predictable responsive behavior. Decoration must not compete with capture, writing, privacy status, or navigation.

## Authorship through accumulation

The environment begins clean and gains meaningful evidence of use:

- Day 1: mostly clean, with a chosen accent
- Week 1: a few traces and placed pieces
- Month 1: emerging monthly history
- Month 3: meaningful bookmarks, photographs, and organization
- Month 6: unmistakably inhabited by this person

The user authors this transformation through content and choices. Do not manufacture personality through unlock economies, compulsory collectibles, or noisy default decoration.

## Anti-pattern test

Reject a direction if it reads primarily as:

- Beige SaaS with notebook lines
- Productivity dashboard with paper texture
- Childish kawaii product
- Scrapbook/Pinterest maximalism
- Cultural pastiche
- Generic cards, pills, sidebars, KPI tiles, glassmorphism, or glowing gradients
- Skeuomorphism that does not improve understanding
- A visual system that becomes less usable as it becomes more “personal”
